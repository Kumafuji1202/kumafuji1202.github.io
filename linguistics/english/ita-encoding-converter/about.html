The Initial Teaching Alphabet (ITA) is an alternative English alphabet and spelling systems* (not a spelling reform, which attempts to displace the current traditional orthography (abbreviated “TO” in ITA materials)) devised by Sir James Pitman. It attempts to work as a stepping stone for children to literacy in TO, albeit not without controversy. Children were supposed to read and write first in ITA, and then transition to reading and writing in TO.

To serve this purpose, the ITA is designed to be far easier than TO to learn, while not being too drastically different from TO:

  • The pronunciation is far easier to derive from the spelling, with only word stress and some cases involving the “r” sound remaining ambiguous.
  • There are some letters with the same sounds, which is deliberately done to facilitate transition to TO.
  • Unlike the Shavian and Deseret alphabets, ITA is an extended version of the Latin script, and not an entirely new writing system. The letters all represent one of its primary sound value in TO (for example, the letter “u” in ITA represents the sound of “u” as in “hut”).
  • New, unambiguous letters are added for sounds that lack single letters for them, and they resemble the usual TO spelling of the sounds (for example, the letter for the “aw” sound of “fraud” is shaped like “au”).
  • There are fewer letter-forms to memorize:
    • The letters Q and X are not used.
    • Cursive script is not used.
    • Capitals, whose shapes can differ considerably from minuscules, are either replaced with enlarged minuscules or the minuscules themselves.
    • There are stricter restriction of letter-forms (for example, the letter “g” must have a one-storey form) and essential shapes of letters differ across fonts by no more than serifs.

The ITA was not supported by Unicode until recently (ver 18.0, released 2026 September), and fonts with ITA support used their own encoding schemes in the Private Use Area (PUA) for the missing ITA-specific letters. This webtool converts text written in ITA between these encoding schemes.

While Unicode 18.0 officially added support for all the letters used in ITA, some of them have been unified (that is, assigned the same codepoints) with pre-existing characters with different and uninterchangeable forms (e.g. ŋ (common) with ŋ with a ring inside (used in ITA)), so some fonts with ITA support keep their PUA encoding for these problematic characters. Because of this unification, some (as of now) fonts that support all unaffected letters unique to ITA with perfectly acceptable letter-forms nevertheless remain unusable as ITA fonts.

* Yes, there are multiple spelling standards for ITA. The version initially used and the version currently promoted by the Initial Teaching Alphabet Foundation (not to be confused with the Initial Teaching Alphabet Association) differ significantly, although the set of the letters and the assignment of letters to phonemes largely stay the same.
